Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): Hi Hank, good morning. I think you are coming through now—yes, there you are. How are things in Berlin?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Busy, but good. We have a media launch this week, so I may be checking another screen. I can still focus.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): I appreciate it. Since the Klocko - Jacobi marketing automation project is already closed and won, I would like to revisit what you hoped to solve and what has actually happened since rollout. Does that work?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Yes. We bought because our campaign process was fragmented. The German team used one set of tools, Austria another, and the central team stitched reports together by hand. We wanted a common operating model without forcing every market into identical messaging.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): That balance comes up often. How did you decide what had to be standardized?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): The basics: naming conventions, consent fields, lifecycle stages, and campaign reporting. We did not standardize creative approval. Local teams still need to move quickly, especially for entertainment and editorial promotions.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): What was the most painful part before implementation?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Lead handoff. Marketing would mark someone as engaged, sales would say the person was not ready, and nobody could explain the difference. Also, event registrations were exported manually. If someone changed their email address, we often ended up with two records.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): Have the duplicate records improved?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Improved, yes, solved, no. The matching rules catch the obvious cases. Shared inboxes, personal addresses used for industry events, and contacts who move between subsidiaries are still messy. We have fewer duplicates, but the exceptions require judgment.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): That is useful to know. And the handoff?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Better because we agreed on a score threshold and added a reason code. Sales can see whether an action came from a download, webinar, or repeated page visits. They still disagree with the threshold sometimes, but at least the disagreement is visible.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): What did adoption look like across the markets?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Central and Germany adopted quickly. Austria was slower because two people were covering the old system and the new one. The Czech team uses the platform for newsletters and reporting but has not embraced automated journeys. They prefer manual control.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): Did training address that preference?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Partly. Training showed the features, but not enough examples from their day-to-day campaigns. A generic “build a nurture” exercise did not feel relevant to a local content calendar.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): We can create a market-specific workshop. What would be a good example?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): A film-festival announcement with different audience segments and a short approval chain. If the team sees how to reuse a journey while retaining local edits, they may trust it more.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): Makes sense. Are there reporting questions still open?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Attribution is the big one. The dashboard is consistent, but the executives want to know whether a campaign influenced a subscription, a renewal, or only an inquiry. We can show touches, but they sometimes ask for a single “winning campaign,” which is not always honest.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): How are you handling that conversation?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): We show first touch, last touch, and assisted influence. For large launches, we add a notes field explaining offline promotion. It is less glamorous than a single number, but it prevents the team from overclaiming.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): That is a mature approach. Has the system changed your planning cadence?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Yes. Before, we planned in channels. Now we plan around audiences and stages. The downside is that planning takes longer at the beginning because someone has to define the audience properly.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): Longer by how much?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): For a new campaign, perhaps an extra day. After that, execution is faster. For urgent announcements, the old manual method can still be quicker because everyone knows the shortcuts.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): So automation is not automatically the fastest path for every request.

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): Exactly. If the campaign runs once and changes hourly, automation may create overhead. If it repeats or has several branches, the investment pays back.

Luis Romero (Customer Success Manager): What would you want from us in the next quarter?

Hank Jaskolski (Marketing Operations): A practical workshop for Austria and Czechia, a review of duplicate-handling rules, and help designing an executive attribution view. Also, someone should look at our permission setup. A few local managers cannot see the reports they need, while a couple of central users see too much.
