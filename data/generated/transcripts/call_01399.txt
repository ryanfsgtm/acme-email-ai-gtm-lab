Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Maiya, thanks for joining. I’d like to use this planning session to identify implementation dependencies, procurement handoffs, and anything that could change the evaluation timeline.

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Sounds good. I only have about half an hour, so I’m hoping we can leave with owners. We’ve had too many projects where “procurement will handle it” meant nobody knew what the next step was.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Agreed. The opportunity for Watsica, Cole and Bauch is still in evaluation, and the current target is a marketing automation program for the retail organization. Before we discuss dates, what does your internal process require?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): First we need a business sponsor, then security and legal review, then a commercial comparison. Depending on the spend, the sourcing committee may ask for a formal bid process. The amount under discussion is significant, so I don’t want to promise that a preferred vendor can simply move to signature.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Understood. Is there already a sponsor?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Marketing operations is sponsoring it, with a digital commerce executive involved. They want to improve segmentation and coordinate messages across stores, loyalty, and online activity. Procurement is not deciding whether the capability is useful; we’re making sure the purchase is competitive, documented, and contractually workable.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What implementation assumptions are they making today?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): They think the first use case can be loyalty onboarding. That sounds contained, but the data is not. Customer identity comes from one system, transactions from another, store activity from a third, and consent is maintained in several places. If the design assumes a single clean profile, the timeline will slip.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That’s a key dependency. Would you prefer a technical discovery before the commercial comparison?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Yes, but it has to be bounded. We don’t need a six-week architecture exercise before anyone knows the price. We need enough detail to identify what is standard, what requires integration work, and what the customer team must own.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We can propose a two-session discovery: one for data and identity, one for journeys and governance. After that, we can provide a phased plan and assumptions. Who should attend?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Marketing operations, data engineering, privacy, and someone from store technology. I’ll attend for commercial questions. Security may delegate an architect rather than the main reviewer. Please don’t schedule all of them for two hours if ninety minutes will do.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’ll keep the first session to ninety minutes. For the loyalty onboarding pilot, what would count as complete?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): A customer enters with a valid consent status, receives the correct welcome path, and can be suppressed if they opt out. The team should be able to see the source of the audience and the reason for each branch. If an analyst has to reconcile three spreadsheets after every send, it isn’t complete.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What about store activity and transaction data? Are those required for phase one?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Transaction data is probably phase one. Store activity may be phase two because the event quality is inconsistent. I want that written down. Business users often say “omnichannel” and later treat every channel as a launch dependency.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That’s a good counterweight. We can define a minimum viable data set and mark store events as a later dependency. How does your sourcing committee evaluate proposals?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): They look at total cost, implementation risk, security posture, scalability, and exit terms. They don’t only choose the lowest price. But if two vendors are close, the one with clearer assumptions and fewer unpriced services usually gets a better hearing.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Then our proposal should separate subscription, implementation, and optional services. Are there preferred contract terms?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): We have standard language on renewal, liability, confidentiality, data processing, and audit rights. The data-processing schedule will be reviewed by legal and privacy. Please don’t describe it as a procurement formality; it can become the longest part.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We’ll send the standard terms early. Are there specific audit or exit requirements for retail data?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Data export in a usable format, deletion confirmation, and reasonable assistance at termination. Our legal team will decide the exact wording. Also, if subcontractors are involved in processing, we need a current list and notice terms.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’ll add those to the information request. On the implementation side, does your team expect a fixed launch date?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): The sponsor has a seasonal goal, but it isn’t a hard date yet. They’d like to be live before the holiday planning cycle. That is ambitious. If they insist on a date before data ownership is settled, I’ll push back.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What would be the most likely cause of delay?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Not the number of users. The difficult part is agreeing which team owns identity resolution and consent exceptions. Also, store technology has a backlog. If everyone assumes their feed is ready because it exists somewhere, we’ll discover the gap during testing.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We can make readiness criteria explicit: sample data, field definitions, update frequency, error handling, and a named owner. Would procurement use that in the evaluation record?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Yes. It helps us compare vendors fairly. One vendor may promise a fast launch by quietly excluding the hard parts; another may show a longer but more credible plan. I need the comparison to expose that difference.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Are you comparing us with a named competitor?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): No formal competitor is listed yet. There are a couple of platforms marketing to the team, but the shortlist isn’t final. I don’t want the process framed around beating a logo. It should be about the requirements and risk.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That works for us. Let’s review proposed milestones: discovery in June, documented requirements after the sessions, commercial proposal with assumptions, internal security and legal review, sourcing committee, then implementation planning.

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Put data readiness before the sourcing committee if possible. The committee can approve a commercial direction, but it shouldn’t approve a schedule based on imaginary feeds. We can run the streams in parallel, though.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): So the decision can proceed while technical validation continues, provided the proposal clearly labels dependencies.

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Correct. And don’t treat procurement as the final step. We need to be involved before the scope is politically fixed. Otherwise people ask us to negotiate a number for a package that was never defined.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’ll include you in the scope review. What should I send after this call?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): A one-page implementation outline, a data dependency checklist, the commercial breakdown, standard security and privacy materials, and a responsibility matrix. Keep the first version readable. We can request the deeper technical appendices afterward.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’ll send that tomorrow. Could we schedule the data session for next Tuesday?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Tuesday morning is bad; store technology has its operating review. Tuesday afternoon might work, but I need to confirm with privacy. Send two options and I’ll coordinate.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Will do. Anything I’ve missed?

Maiya Anderson (Procurement): Just one thing: the opportunity is evaluation, not approval. The internal excitement is real, but we still have gates. If your forecast says closed because the sponsor likes the demo, finance will notice.
