Moshe Johns (Revenue Operations): We have heard that phrase. I am not sure we need a maze of branches.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): I agree that complexity for its own sake is not useful. For example, a referral might receive a different acknowledgment than an anonymous webinar registrant, and a lead that books a meeting should stop receiving prospecting messages. Those are simple branches. More advanced branching can combine conditions and send a contact to a different workflow when behavior changes.

Moshe Johns (Account Executive): Sorry, Priya, did you say behavior changes or the CRM stage changes?

Priya Shah (Account Executive): Either can be a condition, depending on the design. We could branch on an engagement score, a form answer, a territory, or a CRM status. The important part is to document why each path exists.

Moshe Johns (Revenue Operations): That sounds useful for our referral program, but our first issue is ownership. Can the system assign a lead without creating duplicate tasks?

Priya Shah (Account Executive): Yes, assignment rules can use territory, service line, or account ownership. We would define the source of truth and then create only the necessary task or notification. A contact who is already in an active sales cycle can be excluded from a net-new sequence.

Moshe Johns (Revenue Operations): We have several service lines, and one practice covers both outpatient and specialist clinics. Routing by geography alone will not work.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): Then we should include service line and perhaps account segment. Could your intake form capture that reliably?

Moshe Johns (Revenue Operations): Good. Another concern is patient information. We cannot let campaign users treat a prospecting record as a clinical record.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): Absolutely. The implementation should define prohibited data, permissions, and a process for removing anything submitted accidentally. We can keep the campaign model limited to business contact and engagement information.
