Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Terrance, thanks for joining. I know we originally scheduled this as a discovery conversation rather than a renewal review, so I’d like to understand how Sales Operations thinks about marketing automation before we jump into screens.

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): That works. I’m between a forecast call and a data-quality meeting, so if the dog starts barking, that is an honest stakeholder interruption.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Understood. How are things at Dickinson and Sons?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Busy. We have several education programs, regional teams, and one CRM that is technically shared but practically interpreted twelve different ways.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): When you say interpreted, what varies?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Lead stages, mostly. One region calls a downloaded guide a marketing-qualified lead. Another waits until a person attends an event. Sales then complains that the queue is full of names nobody has spoken to.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): So the first issue may be operating definitions rather than simply adding automation.

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Yes. We could buy an impressive platform and still automate an argument.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What prompted the current evaluation?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Leadership wants better conversion visibility. They want to know how much pipeline came from campaigns, and whether regional spend is justified. My team wants fewer manual handoffs and clearer ownership. Marketing wants more sophisticated nurture. Those goals overlap, but the measurement language does not.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): How do handoffs work today?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): A campaign manager exports a list, cleans it in a spreadsheet, and uploads it to the CRM. Then an operations analyst checks duplicates. If the lead meets a rough score threshold, it goes to a regional queue. The whole thing can take a day. If a manager is out, it takes longer.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What happens to leads that do not meet the threshold?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): They sit in a marketing list, although sometimes a salesperson picks them up manually. There is no consistent recycle path. We lose the history of why someone was not accepted.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Would a shared lifecycle model help, or is there already agreement on the stages?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): There is agreement in the slide deck. The system is where the agreement disappears.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That is a useful distinction. Which systems would need to connect?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Our CRM, event registration, email platform, and a reporting warehouse. The warehouse is the source leadership trusts, but it refreshes overnight. The CRM updates more frequently, though not always cleanly.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): If the warehouse is delayed, should journeys use CRM events or wait for the nightly data?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): That is exactly the debate. Marketing wants near-real-time follow-up. Finance wants reports reconciled to the warehouse. I do not want a person receiving a campaign email based on a status the reporting team will not see until tomorrow.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Could the journey use the CRM for action and the warehouse for reconciliation, with a documented timing difference?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Probably, but only if someone owns the exception report. Otherwise two systems become two versions of the truth.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Who would own it?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): That is unresolved. Sales Operations can own the process, but not every data correction. IT owns integrations. Marketing owns content. Nobody owns the uncomfortable middle.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What would a successful first phase look like?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): One region, one program, and a clean definition from inquiry through accepted opportunity. We should measure time to handoff, duplicate rate, acceptance rate, and eventual pipeline. Open and click rates are fine for diagnosis, but they are not the business outcome.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Which region would be a reasonable pilot?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): The UK team is organized enough to participate and messy enough to show us real problems. The central team will want to design a perfect global process. That would take six months before we learn anything.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What resistance do you expect from the regions?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): They will say central definitions ignore local buying behavior. Sometimes they are right. A short professional course in one region may have a much faster cycle than a corporate program. A single score threshold could be misleading.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): So scoring may need segment or program context.

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Yes. Also, some of our highest-value opportunities have very little digital engagement because the first contact happens at a partner event. If we optimize only for online activity, we will punish the channels executives care about.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That is important for attribution design. How do executives currently review performance?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Monthly dashboard, mostly sourced from the warehouse. They see campaign cost, influenced pipeline, and conversion by region. The dashboard looks authoritative, but the definitions behind “influenced” are debated every quarter.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Would a definitions catalogue be part of the project?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): It has to be. Otherwise reporting is a negotiation disguised as arithmetic.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What security or governance requirements should we capture?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Regional access, audit history, retention, and approval for outbound communication. We have external partners who should see campaign performance but not personal contact details. We also need to know who changed a routing rule.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): How many people would build or administer the process?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Central marketing operations, two sales-ops analysts, regional marketers, and IT. Maybe fifteen regular users, more occasional viewers. I do not know whether the commercial model counts viewers separately.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can separate that question from technical discovery. Are there any deadlines driving the prospecting stage?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Leadership wants a recommendation this quarter. That does not mean implementation this quarter. The close date in the record is already behind us, so I would rather reset expectations than carry an artificial date.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Agreed. A realistic next step would be a workshop with marketing operations, one regional sales leader, IT, and reporting. We would map the current flow and identify the minimum data contract.

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Include someone from finance if attribution is on the agenda. They will challenge every pipeline number, which is useful if we hear it early.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Good point. What should I send before the workshop?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): A short questionnaire, not a forty-page requirements document. Ask for stage definitions, system owners, refresh times, duplicate rules, and the one report nobody trusts.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): The one report nobody trusts is often the most informative.

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Exactly. Ours is “campaign influence by region.” It changes depending on who exports it.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I’ll include it. Anything else before we wrap?

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): We need to avoid assuming automation means removing judgment. Sales managers still need to review high-value accounts, and regional teams need a way to explain exceptions.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Then the design should make exceptions visible rather than hide them in rules.

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Yes. If the system is fast but nobody can explain it, we will lose trust.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I’ll send the workshop outline and proposed participants. Thanks for fitting this in.

Terrance Stiedemann (Sales Operations): Thank you. And the dog remained silent, which means we have achieved one operational success today.
