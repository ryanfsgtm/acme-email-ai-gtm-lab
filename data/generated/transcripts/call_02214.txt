Priya Shah (Account Executive): Arvilla, thanks for joining. I know procurement is often brought in after everyone else has decided what they want, so I’m hoping we can start with your view of the problem.

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): That’s a generous assumption. Usually I’m brought in when someone says, “We need this by Friday.” We have 169 employees, three offices in Germany, and no appetite for an elaborate enterprise rollout.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): What prompted the conversation now?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Marketing says they need better automation for proposals, newsletters, and event follow-up. They currently use spreadsheets and a collection of email templates. I’m trying to understand whether this is a real requirement or just a preference for a shinier tool.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): What does the current process look like after someone attends an event?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): A coordinator exports a list, removes obvious duplicates, and sends it to a consultant. The consultant decides who gets a call. It works, but slowly.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): Does marketing own that workflow, or do the consultants?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Consultants own the relationship. Marketing owns the content. That division is not written down anywhere, which is why every new system discussion becomes a debate about ownership.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): Would a shared queue with clear assignment rules help?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Perhaps. But we have to be careful. Some event attendees are existing clients, some are prospects, and some are suppliers. A score could create more work if it treats everyone as a lead.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): We can segment by relationship type before applying scoring. What information is available today?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Company, contact name, email when we have it, office, and sometimes service line. The records are not pristine.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): How would you measure success?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Fewer manual hours and faster follow-up. I’m not going to approve a claim about millions in pipeline from a small professional-services firm. If the team saves a day a week and doesn’t annoy clients, that is meaningful.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): That’s a practical baseline. Who would administer the platform?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): That’s unclear. Marketing has one full-time person. IT is outsourced. The managing director’s assistant handles several systems, and I’m not volunteering her without a capacity conversation.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): We can include onboarding and a light administration model, but someone on your side still needs to own data and permissions.

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Exactly. The sales team may say they want automation, but they will ignore it if it requires five fields for every contact.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): We could start with a minimal field set and add governance after the first workflow is stable. Do you need German-language support?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): German documentation, yes. The interface can be English for some users, but the consultants are more comfortable in German.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): What procurement requirements should we know now?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Data processing terms, EU hosting, security questionnaire, annual price increase cap, and the ability to export our data. We will not accept a contract that makes exit difficult.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): We can provide those documents. Are you comparing providers?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): We have looked at a few names, but there is no formal shortlist. “No decision” is the likely outcome if the internal owner does not emerge.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): That’s important context. Has an executive sponsor been identified?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): The managing director likes the idea, but he is not attending these working sessions. Marketing has been asked to investigate. That may or may not become sponsorship.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): What would move it from investigation to a funded project?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): A simple plan: one workflow, a realistic implementation estimate, a named owner, and evidence that consultants will use it. If the proposal begins with ten modules, the conversation is over.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): I’ll keep the proposal focused on event follow-up and newsletter consent. Would a short working session with marketing and one consultant be useful?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Yes, if it stays tactical. They can show you the spreadsheet and the handoff problem. Don’t turn it into a broad transformation workshop.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): Understood. What timing is realistic?

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): We originally said this quarter, then next month, and now I’m not sure. We have a client deadline and two people on leave. Put “timing to be confirmed” in the notes.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): I will. I’ll send a one-page scope, the compliance package, and two pricing options: a small starting configuration and a broader package for comparison.

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): The smaller one is the useful option. The broader package might make people feel positive in the meeting, but it won’t help me approve anything.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): I’ll make the next step the working session, not a close date.

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Good. I’ll try to find a consultant who can attend. If I can’t, we should pause instead of pretending we have a project.

Priya Shah (Account Executive): That’s clear. Thank you, Arvilla.

Arvilla Conroy (Procurement): Thank you. Send the documents, and I’ll tell you whether we have enough internal momentum to continue.
