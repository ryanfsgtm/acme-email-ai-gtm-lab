Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Hi Kolby, thanks for making time on Christmas Eve. I realise this is probably not the most glamorous slot in the calendar.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): No, it’s all right. Half the factory is already in holiday mode, so it’s quieter than usual. I’ve got about forty-five minutes before I need to disappear.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Perfect. I thought we could review how you’re handling customer communications today, then look at where a more structured journey programme might help. Does that still fit?

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Yes, broadly. Just to be clear, I’m not the final business owner. I’m helping procurement understand the operating impact and whether the commercial case is sensible.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Understood. Could you talk me through the current setup?

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): We manufacture components for a few fairly specialised markets. Sales owns most customer contact, and marketing is one person plus an agency. They send newsletters, product updates, event invitations. Nothing terribly sophisticated.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Are those audiences separated by industry or product line?

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Sometimes. Usually it’s a spreadsheet export. Someone checks it, removes a few names, and uploads the list. We’ve had the occasional embarrassing overlap where a customer receives a prospect message, but not a crisis.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): And what happens after someone downloads a technical document or visits a product page?

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): A notification goes to a salesperson. Then it depends on the salesperson. There isn’t a consistent sequence. We have a CRM, but I’m not sure everyone uses the same fields.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That sounds like a good place for automated journeys. We can create an entry condition, add delays, branch based on product interest, and route a contact to the appropriate owner.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): When you say branch, do you mean different paths inside the same campaign?

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Exactly. For example, someone interested in hydraulic assemblies could receive one set of messages, while someone looking at inspection services follows another. We can also branch based on whether they opened, clicked, or completed a form.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Right. Would we need to build every possible path ourselves?

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): You define the logic and content, but the builder guides you through it. There are templates to start from. Advanced journey branching is especially useful where audiences have different technical needs or sales ownership.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): I can see the logic, although I’m trying to picture who would maintain it. Our marketing person is already stretched. If a branch is wrong, does the whole journey stop?

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Not necessarily. You can test individual paths, set fallback actions, and pause a branch without pausing everything. I’d recommend starting with a small number of conditions rather than mapping the entire customer universe.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): That makes sense. We’d need someone from sales to confirm the routing rules. Procurement can’t decide whether a lead goes to aerospace or general engineering.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Absolutely. We normally bring sales and marketing together for that workshop. We can also create an approval step before anything goes live.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Is the approval step a manual email, or is it in the system?

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): It’s in the system. A designated reviewer receives a task, checks the content and audience, and approves or sends it back. The record stays attached to the journey.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Good. Our main concern is uncontrolled sending. We had an agency send a product notice to an old list last year. Nothing catastrophic, but it caused a lot of internal noise.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can restrict who publishes, require suppression lists, and keep an audit trail. We should also discuss consent and regional rules, especially because you sell across the UK and into the EU.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Yes, privacy will ask about that. Procurement will want the contractual position too—data processing, service levels, implementation costs.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I’ll arrange the security and legal materials. From a value perspective, are you mainly looking for labour savings, better follow-up, or improved visibility?

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Labour savings would help, but the bigger issue is consistency. We lose track of enquiries. It’s difficult to prove whether a campaign created anything, because every salesperson records activity differently.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Reporting can show delivery, engagement, conversion events, and revenue influence. We can give managers a view of contacts moving through each stage.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): “Revenue influence” is a phrase I’ll need to translate for finance. They’ll ask what is actually attributable and what is merely correlated.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Fair question. We should avoid treating a report as proof of causation. You can define attribution rules, but they’re assumptions that need agreement.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): That honesty helps. We’ve seen dashboards that look impressive and don’t answer a practical question.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What would a practical first use case be?

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Maybe technical enquiry follow-up. If someone requests a drawing or asks about tolerances, they should receive an acknowledgement, then the right salesperson should know. But we don’t want automated messages pretending to answer engineering questions.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can keep the automated part administrative and hand the technical response to a human. The journey could branch on region or product family, but the actual advice remains with the specialist.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Could it also stop if the salesperson has already contacted them?

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Yes, if the CRM activity or lifecycle status is reliable. That dependency is important; automation cannot infer a call that nobody records.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): That may be our biggest problem, honestly. We can buy the cleverest system in the world and still have missing fields.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Then implementation should include data standards and a limited pilot. I’d suggest one product family, two regions, and a clear success measure such as response time and completed handoffs.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): What timeline would you expect?

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Four to six weeks for discovery, data preparation, setup, testing, and training, assuming the owners are available.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): That’s not unreasonable. I need a written scope, licence assumptions, implementation fees, and who provides the content.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I’ll send those after this call. I’ll also include a simple example of the branching logic, not a massive architecture diagram.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Please do. And don’t position this as a procurement-led project. I can coordinate the commercial review, but marketing and sales need to own the process.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Agreed. Who should join the next meeting?

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Our marketing lead, someone from sales operations, and perhaps IT for the integration questions. Privacy can review the paperwork separately.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Good. I’ll propose two dates in January and include the security pack. Anything else before we wrap?

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): One thing: please show how a journey can be simplified later. We don’t want a beautiful maze nobody understands.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That’s a sensible requirement. We’ll demonstrate naming conventions, ownership, testing, and how to retire an unused branch. The aim is a manageable process, not complexity for its own sake.

Kolby Dare-Brown (Procurement): Excellent. Send me the materials and I’ll circulate them internally. I can’t promise a decision, but there’s enough here for a proper evaluation.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That’s exactly the next step. Thanks, Kolby, and enjoy the quieter afternoon.
