Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Joannie, thanks for joining. I understand we have twenty-six minutes before your architecture meeting.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): That’s right. We need to leave with an implementation outline, even though the commercial decision went against us.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I appreciate that. I’d like to understand what prevented the project from moving forward and make sure we close out the technical work responsibly.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): The procurement committee chose not to proceed. The main issue was implementation risk. Our environment is large, and the proposed timeline felt optimistic.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Was the concern data migration, integration, or internal capacity?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): All three, but integration was the largest. We have several manufacturing divisions, different consent practices, and a CRM with custom objects that nobody fully documents.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What would a safe first phase have looked like?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): One division, one region, and a controlled audience. We suggested that during the evaluation, but the business case was presented as an enterprise rollout.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That’s useful feedback. The enterprise proposal assumed a common data model that may not exist yet.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): Exactly. Marketing wanted a single launch date. IT saw at least three discovery projects before that date.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Which systems would have required work?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): CRM, ERP customer records, the preference center, and an old distributor portal. The distributor portal has no supported API.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Could a scheduled export from that portal have been acceptable for a pilot?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): Possibly, but legal would need to approve the transfer and retention process. The export is manually generated today.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): And who would own the mapping?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): That was another problem. Our CRM team owns account data, marketing owns campaign fields, and privacy owns consent. No one had authority over the complete mapping.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We should have identified that as a prerequisite rather than treating it as a configuration task.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): Yes. The product itself was not the only issue.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Were there functional gaps that mattered?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): We needed regional suppression and approval workflows. The demonstration showed both, but we didn’t see how they behaved when a record belonged to two business units.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That should have been tested with a real example.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): It should. Also, the marketing team assumed a campaign could be changed freely after launch. IT was worried that changes would affect already enrolled contacts.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We could have run that as a sandbox test.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): We asked for one, but by then the committee was already focused on the implementation estimate.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): If the project were reconsidered next year, what would need to be different?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): Start with governance. Name a data owner, define consent authority, document source systems, and agree on one division. Then run a technical proof before pricing the full rollout.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How long would that preparation take?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): Six to eight weeks if people are actually assigned. The previous plan treated those hours as incidental.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That’s a meaningful distinction. Were there budget concerns beyond implementation effort?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): Yes. The annual amount was significant, but the committee could have approved it if the path to value were credible. The combination of license and internal labor was harder to defend.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What did the winning alternative offer?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): It was not a direct feature comparison. They offered a smaller pilot and committed an implementation architect for the first phase. That reduced perceived risk.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): So the decision was not simply product versus product.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): Correct. The committee wanted someone accountable for the messy parts.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’ll record that. Is there anything we should provide now?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): A clean closeout note, the data assumptions we made, and a list of questions we failed to answer. Please don’t reopen the proposal this week.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Understood. I’ll send a closeout package and mark the opportunity as lost with implementation risk and governance gaps as the primary reasons.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): That is accurate. The business still likes the campaign capabilities.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’m glad to hear that, though I know capability alone didn’t overcome the delivery concern.

Joannie Dickinson (IT): Right. If we come back, it will be with a narrower scope and a named owner.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’ll make sure the notes preserve that context. Anything else before your meeting?

Joannie Dickinson (IT): No. Thanks for handling the conversation professionally.
