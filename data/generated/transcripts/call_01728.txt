Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’ll try not to take it personally. I’d like to understand how Hoppe - Herzog currently handles demand generation, where the process breaks down, and what you would want to improve. We can stay at a high level first.

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): That’s good. We are a small financial-services firm, so “demand generation” is really me, one part-time content person, and sales helping when they remember. We need more structure, but not an enormous machine.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What prompted you to look now?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): We’ve grown through referrals and a few industry events. That worked until last year. Now we want a steadier flow of qualified conversations, particularly from mid-sized companies that need compliance and reporting help. We can generate names, but we lose track of what happens afterward.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): When you say lose track, where does that occur?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): A person downloads something, attends a webinar, or meets us at an event. The information gets into a spreadsheet, sometimes into email, and eventually someone forwards it to a salesperson. There’s no reliable history. I can’t tell whether a lead was contacted twice or not at all.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How do you decide which leads sales should follow up with?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Mostly judgment. We look at company size, role, and whether the person came from a target event. Engagement is harder because our website analytics are separate. A click can mean interest, or it can mean someone clicked the wrong thing on a phone.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That’s a fair warning against treating every click as intent. Would you want scoring, or would a simpler routing model be more useful initially?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Simpler routing first. I don’t want a black box sending low-quality leads to sales and then having everyone conclude the system is broken. If we can define a few clear criteria, we can improve them later.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Which criteria feel trustworthy?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Industry, employee range, geography, and whether they asked a specific business question. A generic newsletter signup is not equivalent to requesting a consultation. We also need to exclude existing clients from prospect campaigns.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How is that exclusion handled today?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Manually, and not consistently. Our client list comes from finance once a month. In between updates, someone can receive a prospect email even though they are already a customer. It’s embarrassing, especially in a regulated industry.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What systems would need to connect?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): We use a CRM for accounts and opportunities, an email tool, webinar software, and a form builder. I’m not sure all of those have clean integrations. The CRM is the source of truth for customers, but marketing doesn’t always get notified when an opportunity changes stage.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): If you could see one change immediately, what would it be?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): A single view of a contact’s activity and status, with an obvious next action. Not fifty dashboards. If someone registers for an event, I want a follow-up sequence and an owner. If sales says “not now,” I want a sensible nurture path instead of a forgotten note.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That suggests two connected workflows: event follow-up and sales recycling. How much flexibility would you need in the messaging?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Enough to sound like us. Financial-services buyers dislike overly cheerful automation. We need approval before anything goes live, and different language for compliance officers versus operations directors.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Would your team create those variations, or would you want help building the first ones?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Help with the first ones. We know the audience, but we don’t have much spare time for technical setup. After that, I want to own the changes without calling someone every week.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What does your current reporting look like?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Attendance, email opens, form fills, and eventually sales outcomes—although that last connection is weak. We can report activity, but leadership asks which campaigns influenced pipeline. I can make a chart, but I can’t defend every line.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How would leadership judge a successful project?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Better follow-up consistency, fewer duplicate touches, and enough evidence to decide which events deserve budget. I would not promise a particular revenue number in the first quarter. Our sales cycles are long and a few large accounts can distort everything.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That’s a reasonable measurement approach. What concerns might prevent adoption?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Data quality. We have duplicates, old contacts, and inconsistent consent records. Also sales may resist if they think automation is replacing their judgment. It has to make their work easier, not create another inbox.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How involved would sales be in the design?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): Two sales managers should review the routing rules. The rest can test the handoff. If we ask twenty people to design it, we’ll still be discussing field names in December.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): And who owns the project internally?

Verla Turcotte (Demand Generation): I would coordinate it, but IT controls integrations and our compliance adviser reviews consent and retention. I can make decisions about campaigns; I can’t approve system access policies by myself.
