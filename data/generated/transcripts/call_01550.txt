Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Amya, thanks for staying late—or getting up early, depending on which clock we trust. I believe you’re in ANZ and I’m joining from the other side of the day.

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Late for me, yes, but manageable. I appreciate the flexibility. I have forty-five minutes, and I’d like this to be a real discovery conversation rather than a product tour.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That works for me. Cartwright, Rutherford and Rutherford is a large healthcare organization, and your role covers marketing leadership. What changed that led you to consider marketing automation?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Nothing changed suddenly. That is part of the problem. We’ve grown, added regions, and accumulated systems. Campaigns still go out, but the experience is inconsistent and the reporting is hard to defend.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Inconsistent for whom—prospects, patients, clinicians, internal stakeholders?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Mostly professional audiences and referral partners. We are careful not to treat patient information as marketing data. The issue is that each regional team has its own calendar, lists, and definitions of engagement.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How much central control do you have today?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Governance is central, execution is mixed. We publish standards, but local teams have deadlines and occasionally use tools outside the approved stack. Nobody is trying to be reckless; they are trying to get a campaign out.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What does leadership want from a new system?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): A clearer view of pipeline contribution, faster follow-up, and fewer manual reconciliations. They also want personalization. I’m cautious about that word because it can mean useful relevance or simply more complicated campaigns.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That distinction is important. What are the current handoffs between marketing and sales?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): A lead may fill out a form, attend a webinar, speak with a regional representative, and appear in a spreadsheet. Sales gets a notification, but response times vary. We cannot consistently say whether a lead was accepted, worked, or rejected.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Do you have agreed lifecycle stages?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): We have names for them. We do not have consistent behavior behind the names. “Marketing qualified” can mean a score in one region and a manager opinion in another.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Then standardizing definitions may be more valuable than adding more automation. What would a successful first year look like?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Better response time, fewer duplicate sends, a reliable consent process, and reporting that executives can understand. I’d also like local teams to execute without asking headquarters for every subject-line change.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What constraints could prevent that?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Procurement, privacy review, integration capacity, and change fatigue. We recently consolidated another system and people are tired. A technically perfect platform could still fail if the operating model is too heavy.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Who would be involved in the decision?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Marketing owns the business case. IT owns architecture. Privacy and legal can block a design. Finance will look at the total cost. Regional leaders need to believe they are gaining time, not losing control.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Is there a target date?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): We discussed this year, but there is no committed launch date. If we force a date before the data and governance are ready, the project will become another layer of work.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Are you evaluating other options?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): We have looked at several vendors, but the comparison is not settled. One option has stronger familiarity internally. Another promises more sophisticated orchestration. I am not convinced features will decide the outcome.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What will decide it?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Confidence. Confidence that the data is handled appropriately, that regional teams can use it, and that the numbers will not fall apart under executive questions. Also confidence that implementation support will be available after the contract is signed.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What kind of support matters most?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Architecture and operating model first. We can write emails. We need help deciding what belongs in the system, who owns a field, how consent travels, and which reports are trustworthy.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Let’s talk about measurement. Which metrics are currently disputed?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Attribution and engagement. Open rates vary by region and privacy settings. Sales says marketing claims credit too easily. Marketing says sales fails to update records. Both are partly right.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Would response-time and stage-conversion metrics be more useful initially?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Yes, if the stages mean the same thing. I want a smaller set of defensible metrics before a giant dashboard.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What data should be excluded from the first phase?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Patient data, free-text clinical notes, old contacts with unclear permission, and anything copied from local spreadsheets without an owner. We should probably pilot with professional contacts in one region.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Which region is most suitable?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Probably our central team, because governance is strongest there. But that may make the pilot look easier than a regional rollout. We need a second-stage test with a local team.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That’s a useful distinction. What would you need to see in a next session?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): A data-flow walkthrough, permissions, consent and suppression behavior, integration ownership, and a realistic implementation plan. Please avoid a polished demo that assumes our data is already clean.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I’ll bring a solutions consultant and ask for sample fields beforehand. Would it help to include IT and privacy?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Yes, but only if the agenda is concrete. I don’t want five people listening to generic slides.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Understood. Is budget allocated?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Not specifically. There is budget for improvement, but approval depends on the business case and the migration burden. A price alone will not make this a yes or no.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): And if the project were paused?

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): That is plausible. We have competing priorities, and “no decision” is a real outcome if the organization cannot agree on ownership. I’d rather pause than launch a system nobody governs.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That’s clear. I’ll summarize the risks, stakeholders, success measures, and open decisions. I won’t represent this as a committed project.

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Good. The next step is conditional on the materials and on IT confirming capacity. We are interested, but interest is not approval.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Agreed. I’ll send the summary tomorrow and propose a working session.

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): Thank you. And please include the assumption that regional adoption is part of the value case.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I will. Have a good evening, Amya.

Amya Robel (VP Marketing): You too, Marcus. Bye.
