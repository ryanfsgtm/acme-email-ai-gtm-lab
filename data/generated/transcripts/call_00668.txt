Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): That works. We are a healthcare services company, but our sales process is not especially sophisticated. We have a CRM, email tools, and a lot of manual coordination between marketing and sales.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): When you say manual coordination, what does that look like in a normal week?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Marketing sends a list on Monday. Sales operations cleans it, checks territories, and distributes it. If someone responds, a rep updates the CRM. If they do not respond, there is no dependable next step.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): So the first gap is follow-up consistency?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Yes, but data quality comes first. We have duplicate contacts and old job titles. Automating a bad list would just make the bad list move faster.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That's a fair concern. How are duplicates identified today?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Mostly by email address. Sometimes by company and name. The problem is that our clinic groups use different naming conventions, so matching is not always obvious.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Do you have a central data owner?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): I own the operating process, not every field. Marketing owns campaign data, sales owns activity notes, and IT manages integrations. When something is wrong, it is clear who notices and less clear who fixes it.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That sounds like an important design constraint. What happens after a lead is assigned?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): The rep gets an email and a task. The task usually says “follow up,” which is not very helpful. There is often no context about what the person downloaded or which service they looked at.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Would the activity history be available in the CRM?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Usually, but not always. Some forms are connected, some are not. Also, the CRM timeline can be noisy. A rep may see ten automated events and miss the one useful signal.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): If you could improve one thing immediately, would it be the task content or the assignment rules?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Task content. Assignment is imperfect, but the team can correct that. A vague task creates another round of questions.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What would a useful task include?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Source, service interest, last meaningful activity, and a suggested next action. Not a paragraph. Maybe three lines that a rep can trust.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That is helpful. How many people would need to use the process?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Around thirty sales users, plus perhaps five people in marketing and operations. But adoption is not guaranteed. Some reps keep their own spreadsheets because they believe the CRM is slower.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Why do they believe that?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): They are not entirely wrong. If a rep has to open four screens to understand a lead, the spreadsheet feels faster. The system may be more accurate, but people optimize for the next call.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Would a smaller pilot be acceptable?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Probably. I would start with one service line and two territories. We can compare response time and accepted handoffs before expanding.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What is your current response-time target?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Same business day for high-intent leads. In practice, it ranges from two hours to two days. The average hides the misses.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Do you distinguish high intent from general engagement?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Not reliably. A form submission is treated as high intent, even when it is just a request for a brochure. That creates false urgency.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Then scoring and routing may need to be tested together, not separately.

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Maybe, but I do not want a black-box score that nobody can explain. Healthcare buyers ask detailed questions, and our managers need to understand why a lead was prioritized.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We can make the rules visible and start with a small number of explicit signals. We would also need to confirm what information may be used under your internal policies.

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Yes. We do not put patient information into marketing systems. The records are business contacts and account information only.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Understood. How do you measure success besides response time?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): Accepted handoff rate, meetings booked, and the percentage of leads with a complete next step. I would also track how often sales operations has to repair an assignment.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That gives us a practical scorecard. Are there any timing constraints?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): We have a regional campaign planned for next quarter, but the date is not fixed. If we do a pilot, the data cleanup has to happen before the campaign, not during it.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What would prevent the pilot?

Thad Hirthe (Sales Operations): IT bandwidth, unclear ownership, and the possibility that marketing wants a broad launch before we have tested the basics.
