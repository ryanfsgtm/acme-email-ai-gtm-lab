Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Hi Henri, thanks for making the time. How are things in Copenhagen—or am I remembering that wrong?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Close enough. We’re in Aarhus, actually. Copenhagen is where everyone assumes the software companies are.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Aarhus, thank you. And no worries, I’ll avoid pretending I know the best coffee place there. How has your morning been?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Busy. We launched a small product update yesterday, so the website team is still chasing forms that decided not to behave.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That sounds familiar. Before we get into platforms, could you give me the broad picture of how marketing works at Ritchie - Lemke? I saw you’re a 76-person software company and that you’re looking at marketing automation.

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): We’re small, yes. I’m technically marketing operations, but “technically” is doing a lot of work. I manage the database, email sends, reporting, and half of the integrations. Our demand generation manager writes campaigns, and the head of marketing signs off on bigger things.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What are you using today?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): A collection of things. Mailchimp for most email, forms in Webflow, Salesforce for sales, and a spreadsheet when somebody wants to know what happened to a lead. We also have a product analytics tool, but it doesn’t really talk to the rest.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): When you say a spreadsheet, is that an occasional export or part of the normal reporting process?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Normal. Every Monday I export campaign activity, clean the names, match them against opportunities, then send a sheet to sales. If the campaign has several touches, the matching gets… interpretive.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): So the main issue is attribution?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Attribution is one issue. The more immediate issue is that building a campaign takes too long. We have to ask someone to create a segment, someone else to check the form, and then I’m testing links manually. A simple nurture can occupy three or four days.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What would a better process look like?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): I’d want reusable journeys, visible branching, and a way to test without sending something to half the customer list. Also, when sales says, “Why did this person get that email?” I want an answer that doesn’t involve opening six tabs.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That makes sense. Our platform can centralize forms, journeys, scoring, and reporting, with Salesforce synchronization. In a typical setup, you could trigger a nurture from a form submission, branch based on product interest, and hand a qualified contact to sales with the activity history attached.

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): “Qualified” is where systems often become optimistic. We have people downloading a guide who are nowhere near buying.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Fair point. How do you define a useful handoff today?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Usually a combination of role, company size, product page visits, and a reply or meeting request. A score by itself won’t be trusted. Sales already ignores a few automated alerts because the last system cried wolf.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We can keep the score explainable and use explicit thresholds—say, a high-intent action plus fit criteria—rather than relying on one download. Would your sales team want tasks created automatically, or would that feel intrusive?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Tasks could help, but only if they’re quiet and relevant. The sales director doesn’t want forty notifications for a contact reading a blog post.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Understood. We could route only contacts meeting the agreed conditions. On the reporting side, what questions are hardest to answer?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Which campaigns influenced pipeline, whether a lead source is actually good, and where people stop engaging. We can report opens and clicks, but those numbers don’t tell our leadership much.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Do you report on campaign influence at the opportunity level?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Not reliably. Salesforce has the opportunity, Mailchimp has the send, and the connection gets lost when email addresses change or a person is duplicated.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We’d want to map the contact and account records carefully before turning on synchronization. Are there any data constraints I should know about?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): GDPR, obviously. We have customers across the Nordics and the EU. Consent is stored in different places, and some older records are missing the original source. I don’t want a migration that quietly turns old contacts into marketable contacts.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That would need a consent audit and explicit subscription rules. We can import non-marketable records for history while keeping them excluded from sends, but I’d want your legal or privacy owner involved in the final mapping.

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Good. Another concern is cost. Our revenue is healthy enough, but we’re not a giant team. I can’t justify a complicated system that needs a consultant every time I want to edit a journey.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What level of independence do you need after implementation?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): I should be able to build and change ordinary campaigns myself. For the first setup, I’d accept help with Salesforce and the data model. After that, if I need engineering for every field, it’s a nonstarter.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That’s a reasonable line. We’d scope the integration and training separately from day-to-day campaign work. Who else would be part of evaluating this?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): The head of marketing, our Salesforce administrator, and probably someone from sales. The head of marketing will care about pipeline reporting. I’ll care about whether I can launch something before lunch.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Both are important. If we schedule a working session, we could build one of your actual journeys and show the reporting path at the same time.

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): That would be more useful than a polished demo. Use the product update nurture, perhaps. It has two audiences and an annoying exclusion list.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Excellent. What would make the project successful in the first ninety days?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Two live journeys, cleaner consent data, fewer manual exports, and a report leadership can use without me narrating it. I’d also like sales to trust the handoff again.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): And what could stop it?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): If migration turns into a six-month data project, or if the price assumes a much larger database than ours. Also, our head of marketing may decide that the current mess is tolerable until the next planning cycle.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): When is that planning cycle?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Budget discussions start in June, but approval may drift. We’re interested; I’m not promising a purchase in June.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Completely understood. I’ll send a short recap, a proposed working session, and the questions we need answered about consent and Salesforce. Anything I missed?

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): No, that’s the shape of it. Please don’t call it a digital transformation project in the recap.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): I promise: practical campaign cleanup, not a transformation project.

Henri Christiansen (Marketing Operations): Perfect. Thanks, Amelia.
