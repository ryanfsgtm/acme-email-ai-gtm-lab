Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Hi, Dakota. Thanks for meeting. I know you are joining as the implementation partner, so I would like to understand how your team would approach the rollout and where you see risk. Are you calling from the client site?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): From a small conference room, yes. The room is technically booked for four people, but the table has decided it only supports three. I have about half an hour before another workshop.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That should be enough for a first pass. Conroy, Boyle and Okuneva is a United Kingdom financial-services firm, around 152 employees. The opportunity is closed-lost, but I would still like to capture what made the implementation difficult and what would have been required.

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): I can speak to that. The customer wanted automation, but the operating model was not ready. They had a small marketing team, several external advisers, and a database that looked simple until we asked who owned each field.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What did you find first?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Duplicate contacts, inconsistent organization names, and old preference values with no clear source. Also, different people described the same funnel differently. One person called a lead qualified after a form fill; another required a conversation with an adviser.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): So the challenge was partly data quality and partly process definition.

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Exactly. The product was not the only issue. If we had imported everything on day one, the customer would have automated the confusion.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): How did the marketing team expect the first release to work?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): They wanted a welcome journey, event follow-up, and alerts to advisers. They also wanted historical engagement reports immediately. Those are reasonable requests individually, but together they exceeded what their available staff could validate in the proposed window.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Did anyone identify a smaller pilot?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): We suggested one event program and one audience. The sponsor liked it, but the implementation owner wanted the full database cleaned first. That became a dependency, and the project stalled.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Would a full cleanup have been necessary?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): No. A full cleanup would have been ideal, but not necessary for a controlled pilot. We could have isolated records with valid consent and known ownership, then created a backlog for the rest.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That is useful. Why did the customer not accept the controlled approach?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Trust, mostly. They had previously launched a system with bad data and spent months correcting messages sent to the wrong people. Their implementation owner treated any exception as a future incident.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Understandable, especially in financial services. Were there particular privacy concerns?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Yes. They needed separate handling for clients, prospects, and event attendees. Someone also proposed using adviser notes in segmentation, which we advised against. There was no final decision on the retention period for engagement history.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Did the platform support the required separation?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): It could support the segmentation and permission structure we discussed, but configuration alone would not solve ambiguous consent. We needed the customer to define the source of truth and approve the categories.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What about integrations?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Their CRM connection was technically feasible. The harder part was ownership. The CRM administrator wanted it to remain authoritative, marketing wanted to edit fields in the automation system, and the external advisers had their own spreadsheets. We could synchronize fields, but we could not make three teams agree about which value wins.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Did implementation cost contribute to the lost outcome?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Yes, but not in the simple sense of the number being too high. The recurring opportunity was 16,095, and the customer worried that services could become unpredictable. We gave them an estimate, but without approved scope they saw the estimate as an open-ended commitment.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What would have made the services estimate more credible?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): A fixed discovery phase with concrete outputs: field inventory, consent map, role matrix, one journey design, and a migration sample. Then a second approval for build. They did not want to pay for discovery, but they also wanted certainty before discovery. Those positions were hard to reconcile.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Did they have enough internal capacity?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Not really. The marketing lead was also responsible for events. The CRM administrator was shared with operations. The sponsor expected the partner to handle decisions that only the customer could make.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Could training have solved that?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Training would help with usage, not ownership. We could teach someone how to build a segment, but not decide whether an event attendee should receive a product message three months later.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That distinction is often missed. Were there any positive signs?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): The team understood the value of a single campaign calendar, and the sponsor was willing to retire several manual spreadsheets. Also, the small size of the marketing team could have made adoption faster if the first release had been narrow.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Interesting. So being an SMB account did not automatically mean an easier deployment.

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Correct. Smaller teams can have fewer layers, but they also have fewer people to absorb the work. A 152-person company may have one person performing five roles. That can make decisions quicker, or make every decision wait for one person.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What competitor or alternative did they choose?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): I was not involved in the final vendor decision, so I should not speculate. I only know the automation opportunity was closed-lost after the implementation concerns remained unresolved.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Fair. If we had restarted the process, what would you recommend in the first two weeks?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): First, name one accountable customer owner with authority to make data and process decisions. Second, agree on three audience definitions and their consent rules. Third, select a pilot source with clean enough records. Fourth, decide what advisers are allowed to see. Only then would I estimate the build accurately.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): And what should the seller avoid promising?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Do not promise a rapid full migration, perfect historical reporting, or automatic data cleanup. Say that the platform can support those outcomes when the inputs and governance are ready. Otherwise the customer hears a guarantee.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That is clear. Is there anything the implementation partner could have done differently?

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): We could have surfaced the decision log earlier and made unresolved items visible to the executive sponsor. We were too focused on solving technical questions in working sessions. The sponsor heard 'configuration underway' when the real status was 'business rules not approved.'

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That is an important communication failure.

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Yes. A project can appear active because meetings continue, while no decision is actually closing.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): I have enough for the review. To summarize: the loss was driven by readiness, confidence in the data, unclear ownership, and uncertainty around services—not simply lack of interest in automation.

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): That is my assessment. They did see value, but value was not sufficient to overcome perceived implementation risk.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Thank you, Dakota. I will document the phased discovery recommendation and the distinction between product capability and customer readiness.

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Good. And please credit the conference room for keeping us concise.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): I will put it in the project notes.

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): Perfect. Thanks, Amelia.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Thanks. Have a good workshop.

Dakota Schneider (Implementation Partner): You too. Bye.
