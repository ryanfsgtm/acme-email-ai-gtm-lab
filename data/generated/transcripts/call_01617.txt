Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Delia, thanks for making time. Before we start, I saw the weather in Toronto is doing that thing where it cannot decide whether it is winter or spring.

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): It is mostly rain today, which is an improvement. I have a window open somewhere, so if you hear traffic, that is why.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): No problem. I would like to understand how Feest - Bosco handles demand generation today, what prompted the evaluation, and what a successful outcome would look like. Then we can decide whether it makes sense to bring in a specialist. Does that work?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Yes. We are an education organisation with several programmes and regional teams. Marketing is growing, but our processes are still fairly decentralised. We have a central team and programme leads who each want control over their own audiences.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How many people are actively building campaigns?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Around fourteen, depending on the month. The central demand team owns standards, but programme staff create a lot of the actual emails and event promotions. There are 858 employees in the wider organisation, though only a small percentage work directly in marketing.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): And what are you using currently?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): A combination of our CRM, spreadsheets, a form tool, and an email platform. We have connectors between some of them. “Connected” is generous, but the data moves most of the time.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What breaks when it does not move?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Usually ownership or timing. Someone registers for an information session, the registration lands in a spreadsheet, and then the programme team uploads it later. If the upload is delayed, the person may receive a reminder for an event they already attended. We have caught it before, but not always.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That sounds like a candidate for a real-time workflow with clear suppression rules.

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Maybe. I do not want to jump to the answer. Some of our programmes have consent requirements and different retention rules. A workflow that is elegant in one department can be wrong in another.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Good point. How are those rules managed now?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Mostly documented in a shared guide, plus institutional knowledge. The guide is long and people do not always find the relevant section. Our data protection officer reviews the framework, but not every campaign.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What is the business pressure behind the evaluation? Is it campaign volume, visibility, conversion, or something else?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): All of those, but the most urgent is visibility. Senior leadership asks how marketing supports enrolment and programme interest. We can report opens, clicks, registrations, and inquiries. Connecting those activities to a meaningful journey is harder.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): When you say journey, do you mean a contact moving from initial interest through application, or mainly campaign attribution?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Both, although our funnel is not a simple one. Someone may attend an online seminar, speak with an advisor, download a guide, and then apply months later. Another person might start with a direct referral. We need to see useful patterns without pretending every path is linear.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That is a sensible distinction. Our platform can model multiple touchpoints and give teams a common view. Would the central team or the programme leads own the system?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): That is an unresolved question. Central marketing would own the standards and integrations. Programme leads would need enough autonomy to launch campaigns. If central approves every email, we become a bottleneck.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We could show a permission model with shared templates and local editing. How important is no-code administration?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Very important for routine work. We do have an IT team, but they support the entire organisation. They cannot become the campaign operations desk. At the same time, no-code does not mean no governance.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Understood. What would your IT colleagues need to validate?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Security review, identity management, data residency, APIs, and whether the integration can be monitored. They will ask who can export data and whether access can be limited by programme. They will also ask about support because a failure during admissions season is not theoretical.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We can cover those in a technical session. Are there particular systems that must be included?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): The CRM is essential. Our event registration tool is next. We also have a learning platform, though that may be phase two. Do not assume we can replace everything at once; the team would resist a large transformation.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): A phased rollout sounds more realistic. Which use case would you choose for a first phase?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Event follow-up for two programmes. They have enough volume to show impact, but the teams are willing to experiment. We would want registration, attendance, no-show, and follow-up status in one view.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How would you measure success?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Fewer manual uploads, fewer incorrect reminders, faster follow-up, and a report that leadership can understand. We would also want programme staff to say the system saves them time. If only the central team likes it, that is not success.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What timeline are you considering?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): We are in evaluation now. Ideally a recommendation this quarter, but procurement and security can add time. Our close date in the record is September, though that is more of a planning placeholder than a commitment.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That context is helpful. Is there a budget range already approved?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): There is an envelope, not a final amount. We are comparing the cost of software with the cost of continuing manual work. The finance team will want a defensible case, not just a list of features.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): If we build the business case around the pilot metrics, would that help?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Yes, as long as the assumptions are transparent. We have been burned by projected conversion improvements that never materialised because the underlying data was incomplete.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We should separate measurable operational savings from potential revenue impact. I will arrange a workflow discussion with a solutions consultant, and we can include someone from IT if you nominate them.

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): I will invite our CRM administrator and privacy lead. Please send an agenda in advance. They will have detailed questions.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Absolutely. One last question: what would make you decide not to proceed?

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): If the system requires central control of every campaign, if the consent model cannot represent our programmes, or if implementation needs more staff than we have. Also, if the reporting is impressive but not trusted by users, that is a no.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Clear criteria. I will reflect those back in the follow-up. Thanks, Delia.

Delia Marquardt (Demand Generation): Thank you. The conversation was useful. We are interested, but we need to test the operational reality, not just see a polished demo.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That is exactly what the next session should do. I will send the notes and proposed times tomorrow.
