Amelia Hart (Account Executive): I can see how that would create confusion. Our proposal was based on the audience size discussed during evaluation, but the pricing model distinguishes stored records from active marketing contacts. Did the team receive that distinction in writing?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): It was in the documentation, yes, but not in the first pricing conversation. By the time we corrected it, finance had already filed the quote under “too expensive.”

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): So the loss reason labeled pricing objection may combine a real affordability concern with uncertainty about what was being counted.

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): Exactly. I do not want to pretend the money was irrelevant. Even using eight thousand active contacts, the annual amount was difficult for an eighty-six-person company. But the forty-thousand figure made it feel impossible before anyone checked the definitions.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That distinction is important. What was the approved planning range?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): Around twelve thousand, maybe a little more if we could prove faster conversion. The proposal was above that, and we could not make the business case quickly enough.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Did another vendor win, or did you pause automation altogether?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): We paused. We are using our existing email tool for now. It is not ideal, but nobody had to authorize new spend.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): What part of the evaluation did your team value most?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): The segmentation and the journey view. We could finally see which campaign steps belonged together. Our current tool is a pile of workarounds.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): And what prevented that value from overcoming the price concern?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): Internal confidence. The executive sponsor asked, “Are we buying software for forty thousand contacts or eight thousand?” Nobody answered consistently in the meeting.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That is a painful but fixable communication failure. If you revisit the project, would a quote with separate lines for stored records and active contacts help?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): It would help, but it would not make the budget disappear. We would need a smaller starting package or a staged expansion.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Understood. I do not want to imply that clarification alone would have changed the result. Was there any concern about implementation effort?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): Yes, though it was secondary. We have one person in demand generation and a part-time operations resource. A migration that requires cleaning forty thousand records sounds like a quarter-long project.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Would starting with the eight-thousand active audience and leaving the stored archive untouched have been acceptable?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): Possibly. That option was mentioned late, after finance had already formed its view. We also worried that dormant records would be counted later when someone imported them.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We should have made the expansion rules explicit. Did your team understand that stored contacts do not automatically become active marketing contacts?

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): I understood it after the call. The finance lead did not, and she was the approver.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That explains why the same CRM category can conceal two different objections: inability to afford the package and confusion over the counted population.

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): Yes. If you are using this as feedback, please write that down precisely. We were not simply saying, “No money.”

Sharon Stanton (Demand Generation): We would need onboarding limited to the two campaigns we actually run, a clear cap on active contacts, and a review after six months. Also, no surprise fee for the archive.
