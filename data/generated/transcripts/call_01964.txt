Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Kristy, thank you for joining. I know privacy reviews are rarely anyone's favorite part of a Thursday.

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): It is not Thursday here anymore, so I am borrowing tomorrow's patience. We can make progress if we keep this practical.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Agreed. The account is in technical validation, with Customer.io listed as the competing option. I would like to leave today with a proposed implementation shape, the privacy gates, and clear owners. Does that match your expectation?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Yes. I am not looking for another feature comparison. I need to understand what data enters the service, why it enters, where it is processed, and how we stop communications when a person withdraws permission.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Let us start with scope. Schoen Group has about 1,738 employees across Australia and New Zealand, and the initial program is for retail marketing. Which audiences are in phase one?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Loyalty members who have opted into email, online shoppers who have consented to promotional communications, and a small group of store managers for operational notices. Those are not interchangeable audiences. The operational notices should not be mixed into marketing journeys.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Understood. We can create separate communication categories and permission rules. Would you prefer store managers excluded entirely from the first build?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Not necessarily. I prefer a narrow pilot with an explicit purpose. If the team wants to test a store-manager notice, document the lawful basis and keep the template distinct.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That makes sense. What systems would provide the data?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): The loyalty platform, the ecommerce system, and our CRM. There is also a customer-service platform with suppression notes. That last one is important because an agent may record “do not contact” before the central preference store catches up.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We should make the suppression feed a blocking input, not merely an informational field. How quickly does it need to propagate?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): For marketing sends, our expectation is as close to real time as reasonably possible. I will not approve a design that assumes a weekly batch is enough. A person can change a preference at lunch and receive a campaign that evening.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We can propose event-driven updates and define a fallback if the feed is unavailable. I want to be precise about the fallback behavior; the system should fail closed for suppressed contacts.

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Good. “Fail closed” needs to appear in the design document, not just in this conversation.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): I will include it. On retention, are there limits we need to configure?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Yes. We have different retention schedules for transactional records, consent evidence, and campaign activity. I need confirmation of default retention, deletion handling, and whether backups are covered by the same process.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): I will bring in our privacy specialist and security lead. I do not want to guess about backups. Can you also clarify whether pseudonymized event data is acceptable for behavioral segmentation?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Potentially, if re-identification is controlled and the purpose is documented. However, pseudonymized is not anonymous. People sometimes use those words as if they are magic words.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): Completely fair. We will describe the identifiers and access controls rather than rely on the label.

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Another concern is internal access. Marketing agencies help us with creative and campaign operations. They should not automatically see the full customer profile just because they need to edit a message.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We can separate content permissions from audience and export permissions. Do agencies need production access on day one?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): No. They can submit content for review. Our internal team should publish the first journeys until we understand the controls.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That is a sensible rollout boundary. How many journeys would you want in the pilot?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Three: welcome, post-purchase education, and a lapsed-customer re-engagement test. The last one is sensitive. We need to define when someone qualifies and ensure people with service complaints are excluded.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We can use a customer-service exclusion flag and an age threshold for lapsed status. Would the campaign team own those rules?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Marketing proposes them, privacy reviews the purpose and controls, and customer service owns the complaint exclusion. If one team silently changes the rule, the approval record should show it.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We can version the journey and retain the approval history. The commercial proposal is 40,839, so implementation effort and ongoing administration need to be explicit. Are there resourcing limits?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): The privacy team is small. I can review the initial design and the pilot, but I cannot attend every weekly campaign meeting. Please build a review checkpoint into the process rather than making my calendar the control.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): That is helpful. We can use a checklist for routine changes and reserve privacy review for new purposes, new data sources, or material audience changes.

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Exactly. Also, do not assume that because a customer has a loyalty account they consented to every channel. Email, SMS, and personalized advertising are separate questions for us.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We will model them separately. Is there any concern about Customer.io beyond the feature comparison?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Their team has been responsive, and the product is not disqualified. My concern is that the proposed architecture from one vendor put preference management in a downstream tool while the CRM remained the source of truth. That creates ambiguity during an incident.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We should make ownership explicit regardless of vendor. For this design, would you accept the CRM as the profile source and the preference store as the authoritative communication decision?

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Only if the synchronization contract is documented and monitored. “Source of truth” is not a slogan; it is a behavior during conflicts.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): I will ask our technical team to include conflict examples: opt-in in one system, withdrawal in another, and a delayed event. We can test which result wins.

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Please include a failed-delivery scenario and an agent-entered suppression. Those are the cases that expose the real design.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We have about five minutes. Can we confirm next steps? I will send a draft data-flow diagram, retention questions, permission matrix, and pilot timeline. You will identify the internal owners for loyalty, CRM, and customer service.

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Yes, and I will circulate the checklist to our legal operations contact. We also need a security questionnaire before production access.

Amelia Hart (Account Executive): We can provide that package. The target date in the opportunity has passed, so I will leave timing as conditional on the privacy and security gates.

Kristy Steuber (Privacy): Good. I would rather approve a realistic plan than be asked to bless an arbitrary date.
