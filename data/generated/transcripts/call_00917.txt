Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Rosa, thanks for joining. Before we jump in, are you still in the office or have I caught you between trains?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Office, technically. The construction crew outside is providing the percussion section today, so if you hear drilling, that isn't an objection.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): I appreciate the warning. I wanted to understand how your sales and marketing teams operate today, what prompted the automation project, and what a successful change would look like for a 367-person education business.

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): The short version is that demand is arriving from too many places, and the handoff is mostly tribal knowledge. Someone sees a form fill, forwards an email, adds a note in the CRM, and then hopes a salesperson notices it before the prospect loses interest.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): When you say too many places, what are the main sources?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Website forms, webinars, partner referrals, and a fair amount of direct email. We also have regional events. The event list usually arrives as a spreadsheet after the fact, with inconsistent company names and no agreed owner.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): And what happens once one of those leads enters the CRM?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): It depends who is working that day. Some leads get assigned by territory, some by product line, and some stay in a general queue. The sales manager checks the queue when she remembers. We don't have a dependable response-time report.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Is the pain primarily speed, visibility, or lead quality?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): All three, but visibility is the one that makes it difficult to argue for improvement. We can tell anecdotes. We cannot easily say, “This source produced 40 qualified conversations and this one produced none,” because the source values are messy and campaign names are not consistent.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That makes sense. How are campaigns planned today?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Marketing plans in one calendar, sales in another, and the customer education team has a third. A webinar might be promoted twice, followed up once, and then disappear. There is no shared view of who received what.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What would you want automation to handle first?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Basic routing and follow-up. When somebody downloads a guide, they should receive a sensible sequence, and a qualified response should create a clear task for a salesperson. But I do not want an elaborate scoring model in month one. We have not earned that level of complexity.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That's a good distinction. We could begin with explicit criteria—form type, company size, region, and stated interest—then add behavioral signals after the team trusts the process. How many people would administer it?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Probably me and one marketing manager. Sales managers need visibility, but they should not be editing workflows. We are small enough that one accidental change can affect the whole funnel.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What systems need to connect?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): CRM first, website forms, email, and webinar registration. Accounting is probably out of scope. We also have a learning platform, but that integration is a future conversation unless you tell me it is essential.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): It isn't essential for the first phase. What does the CRM data look like?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Not terrible, not good. Duplicate companies are the biggest issue. “DKM,” the full legal name, and an old abbreviation can all exist separately. Contacts move between organizations, and titles are inconsistent. If automation starts before cleanup, we will automate the wrong assumptions.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Would a cleanup project be approved alongside the automation purchase, or would that create too much scope?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): A focused cleanup is fine. A six-month data-governance program is not. I need a practical rule set: which fields are required, how duplicates are flagged, and who resolves exceptions.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Understood. How do you currently measure marketing contribution?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Leads created, meetings booked, and eventually revenue, although the last one is difficult because opportunity source is often changed manually. The finance team trusts closed-won bookings, but nobody trusts the middle of the funnel.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What is the internal case for changing now rather than waiting?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): We are adding two sales territories and a new professional-learning program. If the process stays manual, the extra volume will expose every weak spot. Also, our marketing manager is spending two days a week preparing lists and chasing status updates. That is not a good use of her time.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Who else will influence the decision?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): The head of sales cares about response time. Marketing cares about campaign flexibility. IT cares about authentication and integrations. Finance will ask whether the cost can be tied to measurable output. I can coordinate the requirements, but I am not the only approver.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Is there a target date?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): We would like a pilot before the autumn enrollment push. That gives us time to learn without putting the biggest campaign at risk. A full rollout would follow if the pilot produces reliable reporting and the sales team actually uses the tasks.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What would make the pilot a failure even if the technology works?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): If the workflows are clever but nobody understands them. If sales receives too many notifications, they will ignore all of them. If reporting requires an analyst to rebuild the numbers in a spreadsheet, we will decide the system is not helping.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): So adoption and operational simplicity are acceptance criteria, not nice-to-haves.

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Correct. And please don't present a dashboard with twelve colors and call that governance.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Noted. We can propose three initial workflows, a source taxonomy, a lightweight duplicate process, and a small set of reports: speed to lead, conversion by source, and campaign engagement. Would that be a reasonable discovery output?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Yes, provided the report definitions are written down. We have argued before about whether a “qualified lead” means a form submission or a salesperson's judgment.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What is your preferred definition for the pilot?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): For reporting, use explicit fields and stages. We can add salesperson judgment as a separate dimension, but do not bury it inside a black-box score. I want to know why a record moved.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That's clear. Any compliance or regional requirements we should include?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): We operate across Benelux, so consent language and language preferences matter. Dutch, French, and English communications are not interchangeable. We also need an easy way to stop a sequence when someone replies or opts out.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We'll include those in the workflow design. For the next step, I'll send a discovery summary and a proposed pilot plan. Could you bring the head of sales and IT to a requirements session?

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Yes, but send the summary first. If they see a polished demo before agreeing on the process, we will spend the meeting discussing buttons.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Fair. I'll send the written version, then we'll schedule the working session.

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Good. If you can help us make the process boring and dependable, you will have my attention.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Boring and dependable is an excellent sales brief. Thanks, Rosa.

Rosa Witting (Sales Operations): Thank you, Marcus. And good luck competing with the drill.
