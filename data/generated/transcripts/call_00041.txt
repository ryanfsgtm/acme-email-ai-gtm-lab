Richard Treutel (Procurement): Both, but internal work was the louder issue. The quote was about twenty-two thousand euros in annual recurring value, correct?

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Yes, the opportunity amount was 22,033. The package included automation, onboarding assistance, and the standard support level.

Richard Treutel (Procurement): Customer.io came in with a simpler initial package. Their salesperson also promised that our agency could handle most of the setup. We already use an agency for paid media and email production, so that promise carried weight, even if it was not fully tested.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Understood. Let us reconstruct the technical side. Your source data was coming from the commerce platform and the loyalty database, with email engagement from your existing provider?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): That was the plan. The commerce feed was available, but the loyalty export was not real time. It ran overnight. Someone on our side kept saying, “real time,” and then the data engineer clarified it was daily. That caused a bit of confusion.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That would affect abandoned-cart timing and suppression rules. Did anyone document the overnight limitation?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): It was in a spreadsheet, I think. Not in the executive summary. That is one lesson here: the people approving the purchase did not see the same constraints as the people building it.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What would the first useful journey have been?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): A welcome series for new loyalty members, then a second branch based on first purchase. Marketing wanted three messages over ten days. Operations wanted a hard stop if the customer had already bought something. Legal wanted regional consent checked before every send. The requirements grew while we were discussing them.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Did the platform fail any of those requirements?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): Not obviously. The question was how many people would need to touch the workflow. Our marketing lead could build it, but she wanted someone else to validate data mappings. IT wanted a ticket for every change. Procurement saw a project that could become expensive after signature.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): And what did the agency say?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): They said they could build templates, but they did not want to own the customer data model. That was fair. There was a pause on the call, then somebody said we could “figure it out as we go.” That phrase did not help.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): No, it rarely does. Looking back, would a smaller pilot have changed the decision?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): Maybe. A thirty-day pilot with one country and one journey would have made the work visible. By the time we discussed a pilot, the buying committee wanted a full-year commitment and a launch date before the summer sale.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Was the summer sale the deadline or just the preferred date?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): Preferred, but it became treated as fixed. We also had a new head of marketing joining. She did not want to inherit a system she had not selected.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That sounds like a change in sponsorship.

Richard Treutel (Procurement): Exactly. The original sponsor liked the product. The new sponsor wanted proof of reporting and a very short implementation. Customer.io appeared safer because their proposal was less ambitious.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What reporting did the new sponsor ask for?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): Revenue by campaign, repeat purchase rate, and a view that finance could reconcile without asking marketing for a custom explanation. We discussed dashboards, but I do not think anyone showed her a finished example using our data.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That is important. A capability discussed is different from a capability demonstrated. Were there any security or procurement blockers?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): No hard blocker. The data processing terms were reviewed. Payment terms were negotiated. The friction was confidence. We had 157 employees, but only a small marketing operations group, and they were already stretched.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): If we could redo the early process, what should have happened first?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): Map the decision group. Then separate “can the platform do it?” from “who will do it every week?” And put the data freshness limitation on the first page, not buried in implementation notes.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Would you include procurement earlier?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): Yes, but not only procurement. Finance, IT, marketing operations, and the agency all had different definitions of a successful launch. We entered through marketing and tried to collect the others later.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I appreciate the candor. Is there anything about our onboarding that should be preserved despite the loss?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): The workshop where we built the loyalty-member journey was good. People understood the flow quickly. Keep that practical style. Reduce the number of assumptions around integrations and staffing.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I will record that. One final question: is the decision final, or could Balistreri revisit automation later?

Richard Treutel (Procurement): It is final for this budget cycle. If the agency cannot maintain the new system, we may reassess next year, but I would not forecast it. We need to see whether the simpler package really stays simple.
