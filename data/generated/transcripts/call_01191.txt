Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Very relatable. I’d like to understand how demand generation is operating today, what you hoped to improve with the marketing automation program, and where the team might need support. Does that sound useful?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Yes. We have a fairly small team for a manufacturing company. Forty people total, and marketing is, well, me plus a part-time colleague.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That’s a lot of surface area for two people. What prompted the conversation originally?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Lead follow-up. We get inquiries from distributors, existing customers asking about new equipment, and occasional direct buyers. They arrive from the website, trade-show scans, email, sometimes a spreadsheet from sales. The handoff is not elegant.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): When you say not elegant, where does it break?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Mostly timing. A form comes in, I forward an email, someone says they’ll take it, and then the person is traveling or on the shop floor. A week later the lead is still technically “new.”
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): So the issue is less generating interest and more making sure interest has an owner quickly.
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Exactly. And we don’t want to spam people. A buyer asking for a technical drawing should not receive a generic three-month newsletter sequence.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That distinction is important. How are you segmenting today?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): By product family, roughly. We also know distributor versus end customer, but that information is not always captured consistently. Some records have a company name, some just an email address.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Does sales have a shared definition of a qualified inquiry?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Not really. Sales says they want “serious” leads. Demand generation says we need a behavior or a request we can actually observe. Those are different conversations.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What would an observable signal look like in your business?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Downloading a specification sheet twice, attending a webinar, requesting delivery information, or visiting a product page after an email. But we have to be careful. A competitor can do all of that too.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Right, behavioral activity is a clue, not proof. How do you currently measure whether campaigns are helping?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Opens and clicks, mostly. We can see inquiries, but connecting them to an eventual opportunity is manual. The sales system is separate from our email tool, and the names don’t always match.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That makes return on effort hard to demonstrate.
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Very hard. We had one campaign with an excellent click rate and almost no sales follow-up. The report looked wonderful until you spoke to the sales manager.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That is a useful caution. What would you want a better process to do automatically?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Route a new inquiry to the right person, send a useful acknowledgment, and remind the owner if nothing happens. For lower-intent contacts, I’d like a light nurture path. Not a giant complicated journey builder.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): How many people would need to administer it?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Practically, one. If it needs a specialist every week, it won’t last.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): And who should have visibility into reporting?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Me, the commercial director, and the sales leads. Finance might want a summary, but they don’t need to inspect individual email activity.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Understood. Do you have any concerns about adopting a new platform?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Data cleanliness. We have duplicates, old distributor contacts, and people who changed jobs. I don’t want automation amplifying a mess.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That is usually the right starting point: define what can be trusted, decide what needs review, and only then automate. We can map the fields before building journeys.
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Good. Another concern is language. We sell in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. The messages need to be correct, not merely translated by a button.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Who would approve localized content?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Our sales leads, mostly. I can write the English source, but I’m not going to pretend I can judge every German technical phrase.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Sensible. We can keep approval in the workflow rather than publishing automatically.
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): That would help. We also have seasonal pressure around two trade shows. It’s easy to build something in a quiet month and discover it fails when three hundred scans arrive in a day.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What would failure look like then?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Duplicate emails, wrong territory assignment, or a salesperson getting fifty alerts. The team would switch it off immediately.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): So testing with a small group and a simulated event volume matters more than a polished demo.
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): Yes. I’d rather launch three dependable flows than fifteen half-finished ones.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Let me summarize. Your priorities are prompt ownership of inbound inquiries, sensible separation between high-intent requests and general nurture, cleaner reporting into sales outcomes, and an administration model that one person can maintain. Localization and event-volume testing are constraints. Did I miss anything?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): No, though I’d add that sales needs to agree to the process. We can make a beautiful queue, but if they continue using personal inboxes, it won’t solve much.
Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Fair point. Who should be part of the next working session?
Aubree Walker (Demand Generation): The sales manager, one person from service, and probably whoever owns the customer database. Maybe not everyone at once; meetings get expensive quickly here.
