Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Harry, thanks for joining. I know it is early in Toronto, so I appreciate it.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): It is fine. Our west-coast team is the one complaining about the hour, not me.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I have your notes from the evaluation of ActiveCampaign and from the first technical questionnaire. My goal today is to understand the operating model and where a rollout would help. Could you describe the current process from a lead entering the database to a sales handoff?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): It is not one process. Each practice group has its own variation. A webinar lead may receive three messages, while a consulting inquiry is routed immediately. The common problem is that we cannot see why a person is in a particular sequence.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Is that because the rules are hidden, or because the data changes after enrollment?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Both. We have old automations nobody wants to touch. We also overwrite campaign fields when a contact attends a second event. Then the reporting says the latest event did everything.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That sounds like an attribution and governance issue before it is a message issue. How are teams currently building those automations?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): A central operations team builds them. Regional marketers submit requests in a ticket. By the time the ticket is done, the event copy or audience has changed.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can show advanced journey branching, including conditions based on event attendance, account segment, lead score, and time since last activity.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): We can, but I want to stop there. That is exactly the kind of thing vendors show and our team later regrets. We need a controlled pattern, not infinite flexibility.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Fair. The branching is optional, and it can be constrained with templates and approval rules. For example, a standard event journey could have only an attendance branch and a sales-accepted branch.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): That is more realistic. I do not think anyone here has independently asked for complicated branching. We asked for visibility and faster changes.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Understood. We can keep the model simple while still explaining what is available. Where does ActiveCampaign fall short today?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): It is not terrible for small programs. We have about thirty regional journeys, and the problem is scale and ownership. A user can make a change that affects another team without realizing it. Also, the reporting is difficult to reconcile with our CRM pipeline.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What would a useful executive report show?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Pipeline influenced by campaign, conversion by source, and a view by service line. Leadership does not need to know every wait step. They need confidence that the numbers are defined and repeatable.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): And what would help the operators?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): A readable journey map, test contacts, change history, and an error queue. If a branch says “high intent,” I need to know which field made that true.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): The platform can expose decision history and enrollment events. We should verify how much of that detail is retained for your plan. For permissions, could central operations approve regional drafts?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Yes. Regional users should be able to edit within an approved template, but not publish globally. Some teams will resist that because they are used to owning their lists.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can make the approval process visible and set different workspaces by region. I also recommend a naming convention and an automation register from the start.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): We have a naming convention on paper. Nobody follows it after a deadline moves.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Then the system should make the convention easier to follow, perhaps through templates and required fields, rather than relying only on training.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Exactly. Another issue is consent. Canada and the United States do not have identical rules, and our European contacts are in the same CRM.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We should model consent by jurisdiction and channel, with suppression taking precedence. Your privacy team should approve the fields and retention rules before migration.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): They will. Migration is a major risk. We have ten years of contact history, duplicated records, and several inactive automations.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can inventory first, migrate only the needed history, and leave an immutable export for reference. I would not recommend carrying every old journey into the new system.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Agreed. We need an archive, not a museum.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): What would make this evaluation successful enough to proceed?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): A pilot with two service lines. We need to build an event journey in under a day, prove that an operator can test it, and reconcile influenced pipeline with the CRM. If the pilot requires constant vendor intervention, we have not solved the problem.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That is a clear test. We can also include one carefully bounded branch, not because complexity is the goal, but because your teams do have different follow-up rules.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Fine, as long as the pilot shows what happens when data is missing. A contact with no service line should not silently disappear.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can route incomplete records to an exception path and report on them. I will include that in the design.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Good. I also want a realistic implementation estimate. The account is large, and there are multiple business units.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I will separate discovery, migration, integration, training, and rollout. The estimate will remain provisional until we see the CRM schema and the ActiveCampaign export.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): That is acceptable. Our procurement team will compare this with the incumbent, so clarity matters more than a flashy demo.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): I will send a pilot outline, reporting definitions, governance questions, and a migration checklist. Can you provide the two service-line contacts and a redacted export?

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Yes. I need to confirm who owns the CRM pipeline fields first. We should not schedule the build until that person is included.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Agreed. We have two open points: pipeline-field ownership and the consent model. Once those are named, we can set the workshop.

Harry Jacobi (Marketing Operations): Send me the outline. If the director likes the reporting section, she will join the next call.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Excellent. Thanks, Harry. I will keep the next session grounded in your pilot criteria.
