Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Jenifer, thanks for joining. Before we get into product, how are things going with the sales and marketing handoff at Wiza - Heller?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Uneven. We’re a small healthcare organization, about seventy-nine employees, and everybody wears two or three hats. Leads come in, but the follow-up process depends heavily on who noticed the notification.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): When a new inquiry arrives, what happens from there?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): It enters our CRM, usually. Then sales operations assigns it manually. Some inquiries get a call within an hour; others sit until the next day. If the person is not ready, there is no consistent nurture path.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Is the delay primarily staffing, routing, or lack of visibility?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): All three, but visibility is the thing I can prove. I can see a lead exists. I cannot easily see whether the assigned rep followed up, whether the lead downloaded something, or whether marketing sent a second message.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What would an ideal handoff look like?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): A qualified inquiry should be routed by service line and region, with a task created for the rep. If the rep does not act, someone should know. But we work in healthcare, so any automation has to respect consent and avoid putting sensitive details into ordinary email.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That distinction is important. Are you collecting clinical information through the current forms?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): We tell people not to. The forms ask for organization, role, and reason for inquiry. Still, people sometimes type more than we asked for. We do not want that information copied into marketing fields or used for scoring.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We can discuss field restrictions, redaction practices, and a rule that free-text responses are not used for automated qualification. I’ll need to understand your retention and access policies.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Our privacy officer will have the final say. I’m looking for a workable process, not a promise that the system magically solves compliance.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Fair. What are the main inquiry categories?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): New clinic setup, training, product questions, and support requests that wandered into the sales form. Support requests should be diverted quickly. They currently get mixed into the sales queue.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): That creates noise for sales and a poor experience for the customer. How does someone identify a support request today?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Usually by reading the description. There is a category dropdown, but people skip it or choose the first option. Sometimes the sales rep forwards the message manually.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Would a confirmation page with clear paths reduce that misrouting, or do users need to reach a person immediately?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Both. Some users need an answer now; others want a conversation. We cannot put a wall of automation in front of everyone.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We could design a blended path: immediate self-service guidance for common support questions, plus a human escalation route. Sales inquiries would receive a confirmation and then enter a short sequence only if consent is present.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): What counts as consent for that sequence? We have contacts who agree to a call but not to newsletters.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We should treat those as separate permissions. A request for sales contact does not automatically mean permission for broad promotional email. The workflow can limit communication to the stated purpose unless broader consent is recorded.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Good. Our current process blurs that line.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): How do reps prioritize? Is there a formal scoring model?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Not really. Company size and service line matter, but a small clinic with an urgent implementation need can be more valuable than a large organization doing research. If we score only by employees, we’ll miss the real priority.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Then we should explore a score that combines fit, stated timing, service need, and engagement, with an override for urgent requests. We should also make the reasons visible so sales can challenge them.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Exactly. I do not want a mysterious number that nobody trusts.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What does follow-up look like after the first call?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Reps write notes inconsistently. Some use the CRM activity, some send an email to themselves, and some keep a private document. That makes forecasting difficult.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Would a required summary field after a call be acceptable, or would that create too much friction?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): A required field with a few structured choices and a short note could work. Make them complete ten fields and they’ll stop updating the CRM altogether.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We can keep it lightweight: outcome, next step, expected timing, and optional context. Automated reminders could trigger only when a next step is missing.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): That would help. We also have territory coverage across Australia and New Zealand. A lead arriving overnight in one time zone may sit because the assigned rep is offline.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Is routing based on territory, working hours, or both?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Territory first, then availability where possible. For urgent inquiries, we need an on-call fallback. We do not have a formal rota yet.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We can model the fallback as a shared queue until the rota exists. That gives you a safe operational default without pretending the staffing policy is finished.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): I like that. Another concern is duplicate contacts. A clinic may submit a form from a general address, then a director submits another from a personal work address.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We can match on domain, organization, and other fields, but healthcare organizations may have multiple entities under one domain. We should allow a review state rather than automatically merging everything.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Yes, automatic merging would scare me. The cost of one bad merge is high.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): What reports do you need first?

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Response time by category, percentage of inquiries receiving a first touch, stalled opportunities, and the number of support requests misrouted to sales. I also want to know whether automation improves conversion, but not at the expense of response quality.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We can define both efficiency and quality measures. For example, faster first response, fewer misroutes, and no increase in opt-outs or complaint rates.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): Add customer satisfaction for the support diversion path. A fast wrong answer is still wrong.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): Agreed. Based on today, the discovery priorities are routing, consent boundaries, safe handling of free text, follow-up visibility, duplicate review, and time-zone coverage.

Jenifer Haley (Sales Operations): And a small pilot. I do not want a company-wide rollout before we know whether reps will actually use it.

Marcus Chen (Account Executive): We could pilot one service line with a mix of sales inquiries and misrouted support requests. Success would include response-time improvement, correct diversion, CRM activity completion, and privacy review.
