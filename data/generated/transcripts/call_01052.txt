Michael Hand (Privacy): Consent, retention, and transparency. We provide professional services, and our client relationships are sensitive. Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): How is consent captured today?

Michael Hand (Privacy): Several ways. Website forms, event sign-in sheets, occasional email requests, and sometimes a client asks an account manager to add a colleague. The last one is the dangerous path because it can sound harmless.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Would you require explicit confirmation before any promotional communication?

Michael Hand (Privacy): For many contacts, yes. There are existing-client communications that may be service-related, and those are not the same as marketing. The system must distinguish them. We’ve had tools where every communication was treated as a campaign, which made audits painful.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can separate communication purpose and subscription status. Would you also need regional rules?

Michael Hand (Privacy): We primarily operate in France, but contacts can be elsewhere. Our legal basis and retention approach may differ depending on the relationship. I don’t need a maze of rules that only a consultant can maintain, though.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): That tradeoff is worth discussing. A rule can be precise and still be understandable if the naming and documentation are disciplined. How long do you retain inactive marketing contacts?

Michael Hand (Privacy): That is still being finalized. Our current policy is inconsistent. Some teams keep contacts indefinitely because they dislike deleting records. I’m pushing for a defined review period, but I don’t want the platform to make an irreversible decision before that policy is approved.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Then we could begin with review queues and suppression rather than automatic deletion.

Michael Hand (Privacy): Possibly. I need to know whether suppressed records remain searchable and whether someone can accidentally reactivate them.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Permissions can restrict reactivation, and the audit history should show who changed the status. I’d want your team to test that with realistic roles.

Michael Hand (Privacy): Yes. Another issue is profiling. Marketing wants lead scoring. I’m not comfortable with a score becoming a hidden judgment about a person.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): How would you feel about scoring engagement with disclosed criteria, then using it only as a prioritization signal?

Michael Hand (Privacy): Better, but we need boundaries. A person who visits many pages because they are researching for a client should not be treated as ready to buy. The score needs context and human review.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can make the score visible to authorized users and require an additional qualification step before a sales task is created.

Michael Hand (Privacy): That sounds less alarming. Is it possible to avoid collecting unnecessary behavioral detail?

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): Yes, the data model can be limited. We would define which events matter, how long they are retained, and which teams can view them.

Michael Hand (Privacy): I’d also want to know where data is hosted and which subprocessors are involved. That may be a procurement question, but privacy will review it.

Evelyn Sullivan (Customer Success Manager): We can set suppression precedence so an unsubscribe is not overwritten by a routine sync. That should be tested in the implementation plan.
